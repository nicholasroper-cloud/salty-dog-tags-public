## The Short Answer: Florida Rules Depend on Where You Live

People usually want one clean sentence like "Florida requires dog tags to say X." It doesn't really work that way.

**The statewide piece:** Florida law requires dogs 4 months and older to be vaccinated against rabies by a licensed veterinarian, with a booster 12 months later and then on the vaccine's schedule ([Fla. Stat. 828.30](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/828.30)). Your vet gives you a rabies certificate (and usually a tag). That much is consistent across the state. The statute doesn't require the tag to be worn and doesn't set up licensing, and counties and cities can adopt equal or stricter rabies rules.

**The local piece:** A lot of the details are set by counties and cities:

- Whether your dog needs a county or city license (sometimes called registration)
- Whether your dog has to wear the rabies tag or license tag in public
- Leash rules, beach access, park rules, and penalties

Some Florida counties tie licensing directly to rabies vaccination, often through your vet when you get the shot. Others handle it a little differently. A tag rule that applies in one county may not apply across the county line, and city rules can add their own layer.

So the most accurate answer to "what must a Florida dog tag say?" is this: **whatever your county and city require, which usually involves the rabies tag your vet issues and possibly a local license tag.** Your personal ID tag, the one with your phone number, is mostly about getting your dog home quickly.

## Three Different Tags, Three Different Jobs

Dog owners mix these up all the time. On the collar they're all small, jingly, and easy to lose behind the sofa.

### 1. The Rabies Tag

Your veterinarian usually gives you this when your dog is vaccinated. It typically shows a tag number connected to your dog's vaccination record, along with info that identifies the clinic or issuing agency.

Its job is proving your dog is vaccinated, or at least giving someone a way to look it up. If your dog bites someone or ends up at a shelter, that number matters. Whether your dog is required to *wear* it is usually a local question, so check with your county.

### 2. The County or City License Tag

If your area requires a license, you'll typically get a tag with a license number. Some places issue it through your vet, some through animal services, some online. It shows your dog is registered locally, and in many places it also helps animal services contact you.

Fees, renewal schedules, and whether the tag has to be worn all vary. This is the tag people most often don't know they need.

### 3. Your Personal ID Tag

This is the one you design, and the one we make. It has your phone number, your dog's name, and whatever other useful (or unhinged) information you want to add.

As far as we know, Florida doesn't have a statewide rule spelling out the exact wording of a personal ID tag. Even so, it's the tag most likely to get your dog home fast. A neighbor at 9 p.m. can't call the county records office. They can call the number on the tag.

## How to Check Your Local Florida Requirements

Since the rules are local, here's where to look:

- **Your county animal services website.** Search "[your county] animal services dog license" or "[your county] pet licensing." Look for licensing, rabies, and tag sections.
- **Your city's code of ordinances.** If you live inside city limits, your city may have its own animal rules. Most Florida cities post their codes online.
- **Your veterinarian.** Vets in your area deal with local rabies and licensing requirements every day. Ask them directly: "Does my county require a license, and does my dog need to wear the tag?"
- **Call animal services.** It's free, it's quick, and you'll get an answer that applies to your actual address instead of a guess from a forum.

If you move, even just across a county line, check again. Your old county's rules don't come with you.

## What Your Personal Dog Tag Should Say

The legal tags are about compliance. Your ID tag is about getting your dog back. Here's what we'd put on it, roughly in order of importance.

### Your Phone Number (Non-Negotiable)

This is the most important thing on the tag. If you only have room for one line, it's this one.

- Use a number you actually answer, including from unknown callers.
- Add a second number if you can, like a partner, family member, or trusted neighbor.
- Update the tag when your number changes. A tag with a dead number is jewelry.

### Your Dog's Name (Usually, With a Caveat)

Some people leave the name off, worried that a stranger could use it to lure the dog. Others put it on because it helps calm a scared dog and makes the finder's job easier. Both are reasonable choices. For what it's worth, our bulldogs respond to their names roughly as often as they respond to taxes, so we're not sure either side matters much at our house.

### "Microchipped"

If your dog is chipped, say so. It tells a finder or shelter to scan, and it may make the dog look less like an easy grab for someone with bad intentions. Then make sure the chip is actually **registered with your current contact info.** An unregistered chip is a very small rock.

### Medical Needs

If your dog needs daily medication, has seizures, is diabetic, is deaf, or has a serious allergy, put it on the tag. "Needs meds" fits on almost anything and changes how urgently someone treats a found dog.

### City or Neighborhood (Optional)

A short location line like "Tampa" or "Lives on Elm St" can help, especially for dogs that wander but don't go far. It's optional, and it's your call how much you want to share.

### What You Can Usually Skip

- **Your full street address.** Many people prefer not to engrave it. A phone number does the job.
- **Paragraphs.** It's a tag, not a memoir. Short lines engrave cleaner and read faster.

## The Fun Part: Personality on the Back

Once the useful stuff is covered, there's usually room for character. That's kind of our whole thing.

A good back-of-tag line does two jobs. It makes people laugh, and a laughing stranger is a stranger who's more likely to pick up the phone. It also says something true about your dog. A few from our catalog that fit Florida life a little too well:

- *Can't swim. Still at the beach.*
- *Beach bum. Return to sand.*
- *Lifeguard (off duty).*
- *Not lost. Taking a tour.*
- *Outside is overrated. Take me home.*
- *Built for comfort, not speed.*

And for the bulldog people who already know:

- *I snore. It's not a medical thing.*
- *Snores louder than dad.*
- *Runs on naps and spite.*

Put the jokes on one side and the phone number on the other, so the information doesn't get lost in the comedy.

## Florida-Specific Reasons to Get the Tag Right

Florida has some conditions that make a good ID tag especially worth having:

- **Heat.** A lost dog in Florida summer, especially a flat-faced breed like ours, can get into trouble fast. A tag that gets a phone call in minutes instead of hours matters.
- **Storm season.** Evacuations, shelters, and broken fences are part of Florida life. Dogs get separated in chaos. Keep tags and microchip info current before storm season, not during it.
- **Beaches, parks, and boats.** Dog-friendly beaches and parks often have their own rules, and some may ask about vaccination or licensing. Panama City Beach's official dog beach page, for example, requires dogs to be current on shots and wear ID tags ([pcbfl.gov](https://pcbfl.gov/about-us/visitors/dogs)). Check the specific beach or park before you go.
- **Snowbirds and movers.** If you split time between Florida and somewhere else, use a phone number that works in both places, and check licensing in each.

## A Note on Tag Durability

Florida is hard on metal. Salt air, pool chlorine, sand, and a dog that rolls in everything all wear tags down. Engraving that fades into a smooth blank isn't helping anyone.

Laser engraving holds up better than shallow stamping or printed tags, which is why we use it. Still, check your tags every few months. If you're squinting to read your own phone number, a stranger won't manage it at dusk.

## FAQ

Still stuck? [hello@saltydogtags.com](mailto:hello@saltydogtags.com). A human answers. The dogs supervise.

**Does Florida have one statewide law saying what a dog tag must say?**

Florida requires rabies vaccination for dogs statewide, but licensing and tag-wearing rules are largely set by counties and cities. We're not aware of a statewide rule dictating the wording on a personal ID tag. Check your county animal services and local ordinances for the rules where you live. This isn't legal advice.

**Does my dog have to wear its rabies tag in Florida?**

It depends on your county and city. Some local rules require a rabies or license tag to be worn, and others handle it differently. Your county animal services office or your vet can tell you what applies at your address.

**Is a microchip enough on its own?**

A microchip is great backup, but a finder needs a scanner to read it, usually at a vet or shelter. A visible tag with your phone number can get your dog home without that trip. Use both, and keep the chip registration current.

**Should I put my address on my dog's tag?**

That's up to you. Many owners skip the full address and use one or two reliable phone numbers. A city or neighborhood line is a common middle ground.

**Can I put a funny line on my dog's ID tag?**

Yes. Put your phone number and any key medical info where they're easy to read, then put the personality on the back. A tag that makes someone smile is a tag someone's happy to call.
