Do Dog Tags Wear Off? Engraving vs. Printing, and What Actually Lasts

Salty Dog Tags · Updated Oct 12, 2026

Short answer: yes. Dog tags wear off. Everything wears off. Your dog's tag lives a harder life than your car keys. It gets dragged across sidewalks, dunked in the Gulf, rolled in sand, chewed by a sibling, and clanked against a water bowl roughly a thousand times a day.

We run Salty Dog Tags out of Lehi, Utah, with seven English bulldogs supervising operations. "Supervising" means snoring, mostly. Bulldogs are basically tag-destruction test units. They're low to the ground, they drag their chins on everything, and they lie face-down on concrete like it owes them money. If a tag can survive a bulldog, it can survive your dog.

So let's talk about what makes a tag fade, what makes it last, and why the method used to put words on metal matters more than most people think.

The Short Answer: Some Tags Wear Out Way Faster Than Others

Every tag eventually shows its age. What matters is whether your phone number is still readable when a stranger finds your dog behind a Publix at 9 p.m.

How long the text survives depends on two things:

  1. How the text was applied. Printed, stamped, or engraved.
  2. What the tag is made of. Aluminum, stainless steel, brass, plastic, and so on.

A pretty tag with an unreadable phone number is just jewelry. Your dog does not need jewelry. Your dog needs a way home.

What Actually Destroys a Dog Tag

Before comparing methods, it helps to know what you're up against. Tags don't fade from old age. They get worn down by contact, a little at a time.

  • Sand. If you're in Florida, this is enemy number one. Sand is basically sandpaper that hasn't been glued down yet. A tag that spends weekends at the beach gets scuffed constantly.
  • Salt water. It's hard on some metals and on cheap coatings. Then the salt dries in the grooves and keeps grinding.
  • Pavement and concrete. Short-legged dogs, flat-faced dogs, and dogs who flop dramatically onto the driveway all scrape their tags on the ground.
  • Other tags. The rabies tag, the license tag, and the ID tag spend all day banging into each other. That jingle you hear is your tags slowly sanding each other down.
  • Chewing. Siblings chew on each other's collars. Some dogs chew their own tags. We don't judge. We just notice.
  • Pool chemicals and sun. Chlorinated pools and constant UV exposure can be rough on printed surfaces and colored finishes.

Any one of these is manageable. A Florida dog deals with all of them before lunch.

Printed Dog Tags: Pretty Until They Aren't

Printed tags have ink or a coating sitting on the surface of the metal. Common methods include UV printing and dye sublimation onto coated blanks. They can look great when they're new, with full color, photos, and gradients. Very Instagram.

The catch is that the design sits on the tag, not in it. Whatever scrapes the surface scrapes the design too.

Where printed tags struggle:

  • Surface scratches go straight through the artwork and text.
  • Tag-on-tag rubbing wears down high spots and edges first.
  • Sand and pavement can scuff fine details, and small text goes first. Small text is usually your phone number.
  • Some printed finishes can fade or dull with long sun exposure.

Printed tags aren't automatically bad. A quality printed tag on a mellow indoor cat that naps on a velvet pillow might look fine for a long time. But for a dog who treats the beach as a contact sport, surface-printed text is the weak point.

Laser Engraving: Changing the Metal Itself

This is what we do, so yes, we have opinions. We'll try to keep them honest.

Laser engraving uses a focused beam to change the surface of the material. It doesn't add anything on top. Depending on the metal, that works a few different ways:

  • Anodized aluminum: Colored aluminum tags have a thin anodized layer. The laser removes that layer where the text goes and exposes the bright metal underneath. The result is crisp, high-contrast text, like silver lettering on a colored tag.
  • Stainless steel: A laser can either mark the surface (a darkened mark created by heat) or cut into it more deeply, depending on settings and equipment.
  • Other metals and coated tags: Results vary with the material and finish. Laser engraving isn't magic. It's physics with a really good aim.

Why it holds up better: No ink is sitting on the surface waiting to get scraped off. The text is part of the tag. A scratch across an engraved tag is just a scratch. It doesn't peel the letters off with it.

Being honest about the limits: Engraved tags still wear. On anodized aluminum, heavy rubbing can scuff the colored finish around the letters, so the tag looks worn sooner than stainless. The letters themselves are bright bare metal, so they stay readable. Very shallow marks can soften with heavy abrasion. And aluminum is a softer metal, so a dedicated chewer can leave dents. Engraving tends to outlast surface printing, but it doesn't make a tag immortal. Nothing survives a bulldog forever. We've checked.

Hand-Stamped and Deep-Engraved Tags

Hand-stamped tags have letters punched into the metal with steel stamps and a hammer. The letters are physically pressed into the tag, so they're very durable. Stamped tags tend to have a rustic, slightly uneven look, which some people love. They're also usually limited to simple text with fewer design options.

Deep or rotary engraving cuts a groove into the metal with a tool. Mall-kiosk tag machines often work this way, using a diamond-tipped or rotary bit. Grooves can be fairly durable, though cheaper machines may produce shallow, wobbly lines.

Both methods can last a long time. Stamped grooves can fill with dirt and grime, which sometimes helps readability and sometimes just makes the tag look like it's been to a mud run.

Material Matters as Much as Method

A great engraving on a flimsy tag is still a flimsy tag. In short: stainless steel is the toughest and handles salt water best, aluminum is light and takes bright color but dents and scratches more easily, and brass looks classic but tarnishes. We compared all three, bulldog-tested, in Stainless vs. Aluminum vs. Brass Dog Tags.

For beach dogs, swimmers, and dogs who live outdoors, a corrosion-resistant metal with engraved text is usually the safest bet. For the couch-bound, almost anything works, as long as it stays readable.

How to Tell Your Dog's Tag Is About to Fail

Check your dog's tag every so often. Bath day is a good reminder. Look for these warning signs:

  • You have to squint to read the phone number. If you have to squint, a stranger in a parking lot won't bother.
  • Edges are worn smooth or the letters look "soft."
  • The colored finish is mostly gone and the text is losing contrast.
  • The ring hole is stretched or thin. This is a sneaky one. A tag with perfect text is useless if it falls off the collar.
  • The split ring is bent open. Same problem.
  • Your info changed. You moved, or got a new number, or the old number belongs to your ex now. Update the tag.

How to Make Any Dog Tag Last Longer

You can't stop a bulldog from lying face-first on the patio. You can cut down on the damage.

  1. Rinse after the beach or pool. A quick freshwater rinse gets salt, sand, and chlorine off the tag before they keep working on it.
  2. Use a tag silencer or a slide-on tag. Silencers wrap around the tag and cut down on tag-on-tag grinding. They also save your sanity at 3 a.m.
  3. Separate the tags. If your dog wears several, spreading them out means less metal rubbing on metal.
  4. Check the ring and attachment. Swap out worn split rings before they fail.
  5. Pick the right material for the dog. Chewers and swimmers deserve tougher tags. Senior couch potatoes can wear whatever they like.
  6. Keep the critical info big and clear. A phone number in large, simple type stays readable longer than tiny decorative script.

What to Engrave So It Still Works When It's Worn

Durability isn't only about metal. It's also about what you put on the tag. Priorities:

  • Phone number first. Make it big and clear. More on this in what to put on a dog tag. This is the line that gets your dog home.
  • Dog's name. Optional, but helpful.
  • A line that makes people want to help. This is where we come in.

A finder who smiles is a finder who calls. A tag that says "Not lost. Taking a tour." or "Don't follow me. I'm lost too." tells a stranger this dog is loved, a little ridiculous, and worth a phone call. Our bulldogs would wear "Built for comfort, not speed." and "Runs on naps and spite." with pride, if they ever woke up.

Just keep the joke from crowding out the phone number. The joke is the hook. The number is the point.

Questions humans ask

FAQ

Still stuck? hello@saltydogtags.com. A human answers. The dogs supervise.

Do engraved dog tags fade?

Engraved text doesn't fade the way ink does, because the text is part of the metal and not a layer on top. On colored aluminum, heavy scraping can scuff the finish around the letters, but the letters are bare metal and stay readable. Check your tag now and then, and replace it when the phone number gets hard to read.

Are printed dog tags waterproof?

Many printed tags handle water fine at first. The bigger issue is abrasion. Sand, pavement, and other tags can scratch through surface-printed designs. For dogs who swim a lot or spend time on the beach, engraved text is usually the more durable choice.

What dog tag material is best for Florida beaches?

Corrosion-resistant metals like stainless steel hold up well to salt water and humidity. Anodized aluminum is a lighter option that also handles moisture well, though it scratches more easily. Whatever you pick, rinse the tag after beach days.

How often should I replace my dog's tag?

There's no set schedule. Replace it when the phone number gets hard to read, the ring hole looks worn, or your contact info changes. Checking it on bath day is an easy habit to build.

Do tag silencers really help tags last longer?

They cut down on direct metal-on-metal rubbing between tags, which is one of the main causes of wear. They also make your dog quieter, which nobody in your house will complain about.
