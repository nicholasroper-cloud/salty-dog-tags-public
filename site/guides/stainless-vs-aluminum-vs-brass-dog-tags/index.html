Stainless vs Aluminum vs Brass Dog Tags: An Honest Guide From a House With Seven Bulldogs

Salty Dog Tags · Updated Oct 12, 2026

Picking a dog tag seems easy until you're staring at three metals that all look shiny in the product photo. Then the questions start. Which one lasts? Which one is too heavy for a small dog? Which one turns weird colors after a summer in Florida?

We have opinions. We live with seven English bulldogs in Lehi, Utah, which means our tags get dragged across concrete, dunked in water bowls, buried in neck wrinkles, and coated in a level of drool that should probably be studied. A tag that survives our house has earned its keep.

Here's the no-nonsense breakdown of stainless steel, aluminum, and brass: what each is good at, where each falls short, and which one fits your dog's lifestyle. That lifestyle might be "beach bum." It might be "professional snuggler." Either way, there's a metal for that.

Why the Metal Matters More Than You Think

A dog tag has one job: get your dog home if they wander off. That's it. It doesn't need to be fancy. It does need to stay readable.

The metal decides how long your phone number stays legible, how much the tag weighs on your dog's collar, and how it handles water, salt, sweat, and the general chaos of being worn by an animal that rolls in things on purpose. It also affects how the engraving looks. Laser engraving interacts differently with each metal, so the same design can look crisp on one and so-so on another.

A tag that reads "Microchipped. Still dumb." is only funny if someone can actually read it.

Stainless Steel Dog Tags: The Bulldog of Metals

Stainless steel is tough and stubborn, and it doesn't care about your feelings. If metals were dog breeds, stainless would be snoring on the couch while everything around it falls apart.

The good

  • Durability. Stainless resists scratches and dents better than aluminum or brass. Your dog can drag it along the sidewalk, chew on it, or bang it against every metal food bowl in the house, and it keeps going.
  • Corrosion resistance. Stainless is built to resist rust. That makes it a strong pick for dogs who live near the ocean, swim in pools, or drool enough to qualify as a water feature. Some grades, often called "marine grade," handle salt exposure especially well.
  • Engraving that lasts. Laser engraving on stainless typically produces a dark, permanent mark that sits in the metal rather than on top of it. It won't peel off like paint.
  • Low maintenance. Rinse it off now and then. That's the whole care routine.

The not-so-good

  • Weight. Stainless is heavier than aluminum. On a bulldog, a lab, or any dog built like a coffee table, you won't notice. On a tiny dog, a big stainless tag can feel like jewelry from a bad decision.
  • Noise. Stainless jingles. Two stainless tags clanking together at 3 a.m. while your dog scratches is a sound you will come to know intimately.
  • Price. It usually costs a little more than aluminum. Worth it for most dogs, but it's real.

Best for

Medium and large dogs, water dogs, beach dogs, heavy chewers, and anyone who wants to buy a tag once and forget about it. Whisky, the first of our seven bulldogs to get a tag, wears polished stainless. Seven bulldogs don't leave room for fragile.

Aluminum Dog Tags: Light, Colorful, and a Little Soft

Aluminum is the lightweight option. It's the tag you pick when you want fun colors and your dog weighs about as much as a bag of flour.

The good

  • Lightweight. This is aluminum's biggest selling point. For toy breeds, puppies, cats, and small dogs ("Small dog. Big mouth."), a light tag doesn't drag on the collar or bounce against their chest all day.
  • Color options. Aluminum tags are often anodized, which adds a colored surface layer. That's how you get the bright pinks, blues, purples, and neon shades. If you're into an 80s synthwave look (we may be biased), aluminum delivers.
  • Sharp engraving contrast. On anodized aluminum, the laser removes the colored layer and exposes the bright metal underneath. The result is clean, high-contrast text that's easy to read.
  • Budget-friendly. Aluminum tags are usually the most affordable option.

The not-so-good

  • Softer metal. Aluminum scratches and bends more easily than stainless. A dedicated chewer can mangle it.
  • Wear over time. Heavy rubbing and scraping can scuff the anodized color around the letters, so the tag looks worn sooner than stainless. Laser-engraved letters are bright bare metal, so they stay readable, but tags that clank against other tags or get dragged on the ground show it faster.
  • Not ideal for constant salt exposure. Aluminum doesn't rust the way plain steel does, but repeated salt water and harsh conditions can still dull or pit the surface over time. A daily Gulf Coast swimmer will put it through a lot.

Best for

Small dogs, cats, puppies who'll outgrow their collar anyway, indoor pets, and owners who want color and are fine replacing a tag down the road. If your dog's motto is "Outside is overrated. Take me home," aluminum will probably hold up just fine.

Brass Dog Tags: Classic, Heavy, and Moody

Brass is the vintage option. It has a warm gold tone that looks great on a leather collar, a weight that feels substantial, and a habit of changing color whether you approve or not.

The good

  • Classic look. Brass has a warm, old-school vibe. It looks like it belongs on a dog named Winston who has opinions about the weather.
  • Sturdy feel. It's heavier and more solid-feeling than aluminum. Some people simply like a tag with heft.
  • Patina. Over time, brass tarnishes and darkens. Some owners love this. It gives the tag character, like a well-worn pair of boots.

The not-so-good

  • Tarnish. Patina is charming until your dog's tag looks like a penny someone dug out of a parking lot. Keeping brass bright means polishing, and nobody has time to polish a dog tag.
  • Possible fur staining. Tarnished brass can sometimes leave dark marks on light-colored fur where it rubs. If your dog is white or cream (hello, bulldog fawns and whites), keep an eye on it.
  • Weight and noise. Brass is heavy and loud. Fine for big dogs, a lot for little ones.
  • Engraving contrast can vary. Depending on the finish and how much the brass has tarnished, engraved text may not pop as much over time. Legibility is the whole point of a tag, so this matters.
  • Moisture sensitivity. Humid climates, salt air, and regular dunking can speed up tarnish. Florida humidity doesn't negotiate.

Best for

Larger dogs, owners who love a vintage look and don't mind some upkeep, and anyone who thinks of a patina as a feature, not a flaw.

The Florida Factor: Salt, Sun, and Pool Water

If you're in Florida, your dog's tag has a harder job than most. Salt air, constant humidity, chlorinated pools, and beach days all take a toll on metal.

For Florida dogs, especially ones who think every body of water is personally inviting them in, stainless steel is the safest bet. It handles salt and moisture better than the other two. Aluminum can work, but expect it to look more worn over time. Brass will tarnish faster in humid, salty air, so only pick it if you like the aged look.

A quick freshwater rinse after a beach day helps any tag last longer. It helps the dog too, honestly.

And a reminder from a family of bulldog owners: many bulldogs can't swim. "Can't swim. Still at the beach." is a joke on a tag, not a training plan. Life vests exist for a reason.

What About Silencers, Size, and Shape?

The metal is only part of the decision.

  • Size: Match the tag to your dog. A big tag on a tiny dog bounces around and can bother them. A tiny tag on a big dog is hard to read.
  • Silencers: Rubber or silicone tag silencers wrap around the edge and cut down on jingling. They're great for stainless and brass, and they protect the engraving from rubbing too.
  • Number of tags: If your dog wears an ID tag, a rabies tag, and a license tag, those tags hit each other constantly. That's how engraving wears down. Consider a slide-on tag or a silencer to keep the peace.
  • Attachment: Split rings work but can snag. Check the ring regularly. A great tag doesn't help if it falls off at the dog park.

What to Put on the Tag

Whatever metal you choose, the essentials are the same:

  1. Your phone number (the most important thing on the tag)
  2. Your dog's name, if you're comfortable sharing it
  3. A note like "Microchipped" if your dog is chipped
  4. Any medical needs, like "Needs meds"

Then, if there's room, add some personality. A tag that says "Return for reward. Reward is me." is still a functional ID tag. It just also makes whoever finds your dog laugh before they call you. That's a win.

Questions humans ask

FAQ

Still stuck? hello@saltydogtags.com. A human answers. The dogs supervise.

Which dog tag metal lasts the longest?

Stainless steel is generally the most durable choice. It resists scratches, rust, and corrosion better than aluminum or brass, and laser engraving on stainless tends to stay legible for a long time.

Are aluminum dog tags bad?

Not at all. Aluminum tags are lightweight, colorful, and affordable, which makes them great for small dogs, cats, and puppies. They're softer, though, so they scratch and scuff more easily with heavy wear.

Do brass dog tags turn colors?

Yes. Brass tarnishes over time and can darken or develop a patina, especially in humid or salty environments. Some people love the look. If you want it shiny, you'll need to polish it now and then.

What's the best dog tag for a dog who swims a lot?

Stainless steel. It handles water, salt, and chlorine better than aluminum or brass. Give it a freshwater rinse after salt water or pool time to keep it looking good.

Is laser engraving better than stamped engraving?

Laser engraving produces clean, precise text and fine detail, and it marks the metal directly instead of relying on paint or ink that can wear off. That's why we laser engrave every tag, including the ones that say "Snores louder than dad."
